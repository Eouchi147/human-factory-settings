# Supplements and nutrition · the deep guide

Sep 30, 2026 · @Sam

A Human Factory Settings guide: what the body needs from food, the few supplements with real evidence for specific people, and the many most people do not need, graded by the strength of the evidence. General education, not personal medical advice.

## At a glance

Food does almost all the work. A few supplements help specific people with specific needs; most healthy adults eating a varied diet need one or two at most, and some popular pills do harm.

| Supplement | Who it helps | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Folic acid, 400 µg a day | Anyone who could become pregnant, from trying until week 12 | Strong |
| Vitamin D, 10 µg (400 IU) a day | Little sun, covered or darker skin, autumn and winter in the north, older adults | Good for these groups |
| Vitamin B12 | Vegans, many adults over 65, people on metformin | Strong |
| Iron | Only when a blood test shows it is low | Strong when deficient; harmful when not |
| Iodine | Pregnancy and breastfeeding, where local advice says so | Good |
| Creatine, 3 to 5 g a day | People doing strength training | Strong |
| Caffeine, 3 to 6 mg per kg | Endurance and power performance | Strong |
| Protein powder | Only as a convenient way to reach a daily protein target | Good |

**Most people do not need:** multivitamins (no survival benefit in 390,124 adults), fish oil for prevention (no effect on heart events in a 25,871-person trial, and more atrial fibrillation), antioxidant pills (beta-carotene raised lung cancer in smokers by 18%), detox products, "superfoods" (a word with no legal meaning), and most herbal blends.

If you take medicines, are pregnant or have a health condition, check any supplement with a doctor or pharmacist first.

## How the body uses food

The body needs energy, protein, fats, carbohydrates, fibre, water and about 30 vitamins and minerals. Europe's food safety authority sets the reference amounts for healthy adults; WHO sets the limits.

| Nutrient | Reference for healthy adults | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protein | 0.83 g per kg of body weight a day | [EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2017_09_DRVs_summary_report.pdf) |
| Carbohydrate | 45 to 60% of energy | EFSA |
| Fat | 20 to 35% of energy; saturated fat under 10% | EFSA; [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet) |
| Fibre | 25 g a day | EFSA |
| Water, from drinks and food | 2.0 L a day for women, 2.5 L for men | EFSA |
| Free sugars (added sugar, honey, syrups, juice) | Under 10% of energy; under 5% (about 25 g) is better | [WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children) |
| Salt | Under 5 g a day | WHO |
| Fruit and vegetables | At least 400 g a day | WHO |

Needs change with age, pregnancy, illness and training. The US guidelines for 2025 to 2030 set protein higher, at 1.2 to 1.6 g per kg ([DGA](https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf)); see Protein in practice.

## Eating patterns with the strongest evidence

The pattern matters more than any single food: mostly plants and whole foods, plenty of fibre, olive oil and nuts, little processed meat, few sugary drinks, little salt, and less alcohol than most people drink.

| Pattern or food | What was found | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mediterranean diet | 7,447 adults at high heart risk, 4.8 years: major heart events 31% lower with extra olive oil and 28% lower with nuts, versus low-fat advice ([PREDIMED 2018](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389)) | Good |
| Fibre | Highest vs lowest intake is linked to 15 to 30% lower death, heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer; the benefit is greatest from 25 to 29 g a day ([Reynolds 2019](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31809-9>)) | Good |
| Fruit and vegetables | Each extra 200 g a day is linked to a 10% lower death rate; benefits continue up to about 800 g a day ([Aune 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyw319)) | Some |
| Ultra-processed food | In a ward trial people ate 508 kcal a day more ([Hall 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2019.05.008)); across 9.9 million people it is linked to 32 of 45 health outcomes studied, including 50% higher heart-disease death (very low certainty) ([Lane 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310)) | Some |
| Processed meat | Carcinogen, Group 1: each 50 g a day raises bowel cancer risk by about 18%. Red meat is Group 2A, probably carcinogenic ([IARC](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf)) | Strong |
| Alcohol | Also a Group 1 carcinogen; WHO Europe says no level is safe ([WHO](https://www.who.int/europe/news/item/04-01-2023-no-level-of-alcohol-consumption-is-safe-for-our-health)) | Strong |

**Alcohol limits differ by country.** Canada: 2 drinks a week or fewer is low risk ([CCSA 2023](https://www.ccsa.ca/canadas-guidance-alcohol-and-health)). France: at most 10 drinks a week, at most 2 a day, and days without any ([Santé publique France](https://www.santepubliquefrance.fr/les-actualites/consommation-dalcool-ou-en-etes-vous)). Both agree the risk starts from the first drink, and pregnancy means none.

**What "Group 1" means:** the evidence that it can cause cancer is strong. It does not mean processed meat is as dangerous as tobacco; the category measures certainty, not size of risk.

## Protein in practice

Europe's reference intake is 0.83 g of protein per kg a day, enough for nearly all healthy adults; people who train, older adults and people losing weight do better on 1.2 to 1.6 g per kg, spread over the day.

| Who | Daily protein | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Healthy adults, European reference | 0.83 g per kg | [EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2017_09_DRVs_summary_report.pdf) |
| Adults, US guidelines 2025 to 2030 | 1.2 to 1.6 g per kg | [DGA](https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf) |
| Over 65 | At least 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg; 1.2 or more if active; 1.2 to 1.5 with illness | [PROT-AGE, Bauer 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jamda.2013.05.021) |
| Strength training | Gains in lean mass level off at about 1.6 g per kg of total intake | [Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608) |

For a 70 kg adult that is 58 g at the European reference and 84 to 112 g at 1.2 to 1.6 g per kg; about 25 to 30 g per meal covers it ([Leidy 2015](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.114.084038)).

**Protein powder** adds a little: across 49 trials of people lifting weights, supplements added 0.30 kg of lean mass and 2.49 kg to a one-rep leg press ([Morton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608)). Food protein does the same job.

People with kidney disease need their own target from their doctor; the older-adult advice above excludes severe kidney disease.

## Supplements, graded

Each supplement with who it helps, the usual dose, the strength of the evidence, and its risks side by side. Doses are general education; confirm yours with a doctor or pharmacist.

| Supplement | Helps | Usual dose | Evidence | Risks and limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Folic acid | Anyone who could become pregnant | 400 µg a day from trying until week 12; 5 mg on prescription if risk is high ([WHO](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/folate-periconceptional), [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/vitamins-supplements-and-nutrition/)) | Strong: 72% fewer repeat neural tube defects ([MRC 1991](https://www.sjsu.edu/faculty/gerstman/hs261/Lancet1991-338-8760-131-137.htm)) | Upper limit 1,000 µg a day |
| Vitamin D | Little sun, darker or covered skin, older adults, pregnancy | 10 µg (400 IU) a day ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/), [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/nutrients/vitamin-d.html)) | Good for these groups. No effect on cancer, heart events or fractures in healthy adults ([VITAL](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944), [VITAL fractures](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2202106)) | Upper limit 100 µg (4,000 IU) a day |
| Vitamin B12 | Vegans, many over-65s, people on metformin | Vegans: at least 10 µg daily or 2,000 µg weekly ([BDA](https://www.bda.uk.com/resource/vegetarian-vegan-plant-based-diet.html)) | Strong | No upper limit set |
| Iron | Proven deficiency; heavy periods and pregnancy on advice | As prescribed after a blood test | Strong when deficient | Overdose is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under 6; harmful in haemochromatosis ([NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/)) |
| Iodine | Pregnancy and breastfeeding | Per local advice; WHO total intake 250 µg a day | Good | Upper limit 600 µg a day |
| Creatine monohydrate | Strength training | 3 to 5 g a day ([ISSN](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z)) | Strong | No harm found in healthy people up to 30 g a day for 5 years |
| Caffeine | Endurance and power | 3 to 6 mg per kg ([ISSN](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4)) | Strong | Up to 400 mg a day for adults, 200 mg at once, 200 mg a day in pregnancy; 100 mg near bedtime can disturb sleep ([EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine)) |
| Protein powder | Reaching a protein target | To fill the gap | Good, small effect | None at normal intakes |
| Fish oil (omega-3) | Prescribed purified EPA for people with heart disease and high triglycerides | 4 g a day, prescription | Some: one big trial helped ([REDUCE-IT](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792)), another found nothing ([STRENGTH](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2020.22258)); no effect in healthy people ([VITAL](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1811403)) | Atrial fibrillation 25% more likely; 49% above 1 g a day ([Gencer 2021](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.121.055654)) |
| Melatonin | Jet lag; falling asleep slightly faster | 0.5 to 5 mg near bedtime at the destination | Good for jet lag; small for sleep (7 minutes faster, [meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0063773)) | France: supplements under 2 mg a day; avoid in pregnancy, breastfeeding, childhood, autoimmune disease ([ANSES](https://www.anses.fr/en/content/anses-recommends-certain-populations-avoid-consumption-food-supplements-containing)). Canada: under-18 sleep use is prescription-only since June 2026 |
| Probiotics | People on antibiotics at high risk of C. difficile | Specific strains | Good for that use: 4.0% to 1.5% ([Cochrane](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006095.pub4)) | Avoid if immunocompromised or severely ill |
| Magnesium | Possibly older adults with insomnia | Within 250 mg a day from supplements | Early: 3 small trials | Upper limit 250 mg a day from supplements |
| Multivitamin | People with restricted diets, on advice | One a day | No prevention benefit shown ([USPSTF](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/document/final-recommendation-statement/vitamin-supplementation-to-prevent-cvd-and-cancer-preventive-medication), [Loftfield 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.18729)) | Check it does not double other supplements |
| Beta-carotene, vitamin E pills | No one, for prevention | None | Strong evidence against: lung cancer up 18% in smokers ([ATBC](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199404143301501)) | USPSTF recommends against |
| High-dose vitamin B6 | No one | Stay under 12 mg a day | Harm signal | Nerve damage reported from 10 mg a day ([EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/8006), [Health Canada](https://dhpp.hpfb-dgpsa.ca/review-documents/resource/SSR1773147434487)) |
| Ashwagandha | Not recommended | None | Early | Liver injury reports; sale banned in Denmark; not for pregnancy, children or liver disease ([BfR](https://www.bfr.bund.de/cm/349/ashwagandha-food-supplements-with-potential-health-risks.pdf), [LiverTox](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548536/)) |
| Turmeric or curcumin extracts | Not recommended | None | Early | A well-documented cause of liver injury; cooking amounts are not the concern ([LiverTox](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548561/)) |

## Who needs what

Needs follow the person, not the product. These are the groups with a clear case, and what each should ask about.

| Group | What to ask a doctor or pharmacist about | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trying to conceive or pregnant | Folic acid 400 µg a day; iodine and vitamin D as locally advised; no vitamin A supplements or liver; caffeine at most 200 mg a day; no alcohol | [WHO](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/folate-periconceptional), [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/vitamins-supplements-and-nutrition/), [EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine) |
| Vegan or mostly plant-based | A reliable B12 source is essential; watch iron, calcium, iodine, vitamin D, omega-3, zinc and protein | [Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics](<https://www.jandonline.org/article/S2212-2672(16)31192-3/fulltext>), [BDA](https://www.bda.uk.com/resource/vegetarian-vegan-plant-based-diet.html) |
| Over 65 (vitamin D from 51 in Canada) | Vitamin D; B12, since 8 to 9% of older adults absorb it poorly; protein 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg | [NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/), [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/nutrients/vitamin-d.html) |
| Little sun, darker or covered skin | Vitamin D 10 µg a day all year | [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/) |
| Heavy periods, tiredness | A ferritin blood test before any iron | [NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/) |
| Training hard | Creatine, caffeine, enough protein; only batch-tested products if you are tested for doping | [UKAD](https://www.ukad.org.uk/athletes/managing-supplement-risks) |
| On regular medicines | Any supplement at all, before starting: see Safety | [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/st-johns-wort) |

## Safety

More is not better. Every nutrient has a level above which harm begins, some herbs interact with common medicines, and supplements can contain things the label does not list.

| Nutrient | Upper limit for adults, per day (EFSA) | What goes wrong above it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamin D | 100 µg (4,000 IU) | High blood calcium |
| Vitamin A (retinol) | 3,000 µg | Birth defects in pregnancy |
| Vitamin B6 | 12 mg | Nerve damage |
| Folic acid | 1,000 µg | Can mask B12 deficiency |
| Iodine | 600 µg | Thyroid problems |
| Zinc | 25 mg | Copper deficiency |
| Selenium | 255 µg | Hair and nail loss |
| Magnesium, from supplements | 250 mg | Diarrhoea |
| Iron | No EFSA limit; "safe level" 40 mg (US upper limit 45 mg) | Stomach upset; iron overload |

Limits from the [EFSA upper-level summary](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2024-05/ul-summary-report.pdf).

**Interactions to know.** St John's wort weakens birth control pills, cyclosporine, digoxin, some HIV drugs and warfarin, and can cause serious serotonin reactions with antidepressants ([NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/st-johns-wort)). People on warfarin should keep vitamin K intake steady from day to day ([NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-HealthProfessional/)).

**Contamination.** In a study of 634 sports supplements bought in 13 countries, 14.8% contained steroids not on the label ([Geyer 2004](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-2004-819955)). The data are old, but anti-doping agencies still warn that no supplement is guaranteed clean ([UKAD](https://www.ukad.org.uk/athletes/managing-supplement-risks)).

**How supplements are regulated.** In the EU they are foods: they can be sold without proof that they work, and the label may not claim to prevent, treat or cure disease ([Directive 2002/46/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32002L0046)). In Canada, natural health products need a licence and carry an eight-digit NPN ([NHP Regulations](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2003-196/FullText.html)). In the US the FDA does not approve supplements before they are sold ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/fda-101-dietary-supplements)).

**Natural is not the same as safe.** Comfrey and kava can harm the liver ([NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/WYNTK-Consumer/)); ashwagandha and concentrated turmeric are current examples.

## Reading a label and a claim

In the EU, any health claim on food or a supplement is banned unless it has been authorised, and most claims ever submitted were not.

1. **Look for an authorised claim, word for word.** Of the general health claims assessed, 229 were authorised and 1,841 rejected; more than 2,000 claims for plant ingredients have been on hold since 2012 ([European Parliament study, 2023](<https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/STUD/2023/747456/EPRS_STU(2023)747456_EN.pdf>); [Regulation 1924/2006](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32006R1924)).
2. **Vague wellbeing words need a real claim behind them.** "Supports vitality" is only allowed next to a specific authorised claim.
3. **"Superfood" means nothing legally** ([BfR](https://www.bfr.bund.de/cm/349/super-foods-and-supplements-risky-or-healthy-abstracts.pdf)).
4. **Check the dose per serving against the upper limits** in Safety, especially vitamin D, B6 and zinc.
5. **In Canada, check for the NPN**, an eight-digit licence number.
6. **Testing seals lower the risk, never to zero.** Informed Sport and NSF Certified for Sport test each batch for banned substances; no seal proves a product works ([NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/WYNTK-Consumer/)).

## Myths, graded

| Claim | Verdict | What the evidence shows | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "Detox products cleanse your body" | False | No randomised trial has tested a commercial detox diet; the liver and kidneys already do this job ([Klein and Kiat 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.12286)) | No evidence of benefit |
| "Alkaline water or diet prevents cancer" | False | Only one study met the criteria in a systematic review; promoting it for cancer is "not justified" ([Fenton and Huang 2016](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2015-010438)) | No evidence of benefit |
| "You need 8 glasses of water a day" | Not a rule | No study supports it ([Valtin 2002](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00365.2002)); tea, coffee and food count; drink to thirst and aim for pale yellow urine ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/food-guidelines-and-food-labels/water-drinks-nutrition/)) | Some |
| "Vitamin D prevents cancer and heart disease" | Not in healthy adults | 25,871 people, 5.3 years, no difference ([VITAL](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944)) | Strong |
| "Fish oil protects your heart" | Not for healthy people | No effect on major heart events in VITAL; more atrial fibrillation | Strong |
| "A multivitamin is cheap insurance" | No benefit shown | No lower death rate across 390,124 adults ([Loftfield 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.18729)) | Good |
| "Natural means safe" | False | Comfrey, kava, ashwagandha and turmeric extracts can injure the liver | Strong |
| "Superfoods" | Marketing | No legal definition in the EU ([BfR](https://www.bfr.bund.de/cm/349/super-foods-and-supplements-risky-or-healthy-abstracts.pdf)) | Not a scientific term |

## Our rules

Independence is what makes a guide like this worth trusting, so these rules hold for every page, film and post about food and supplements.

1. No supplement sponsors, affiliate links or product placements in health content.
2. We name the nutrient, the dose and the evidence, never a brand.
3. Every benefit is shown next to its risks and its evidence chip.
4. Every dose comes with the line: check with a doctor or pharmacist if you take medicines, are pregnant or have a condition.
5. No supplement is ever presented as a substitute for medical treatment, which French law also forbids in paid promotion.
6. Health wording follows the EU's authorised claims when a product is named at all.

Why: Examine, one of the best-known independent sources in this field, takes no money from outside sources at all ([Examine](https://examine.com/about/)), and paid ties are where audiences look first for bias.
